The Seven Med Spa KPIs Worth Tracking (And the Ones to Ignore)

Most practice dashboards track what is easy to measure rather than what changes decisions. Here is a shorter list — seven numbers that, if you know them, tell you where your money is going and what to do about it.

1. Consultation show rate

Attended divided by booked. This is your cheapest early warning: it moves quickly, it responds directly to reminder and deposit changes, and every point of improvement recovers acquisition spend you have already committed.

2. Consultation-to-treatment conversion

Purchased divided by attended. The single most important number in an aesthetic practice and the one most commonly untracked. Measure same-day and 30-day separately — the gap between them is your existing delayed-conversion rate, and it is usually happening by accident.

3. Cost per acquired patient

Not cost per lead. Acquisition spend divided by patients who actually purchased treatment. This is the number that tells you whether your marketing is working, and it can move in the opposite direction to cost per lead.

4. First-year patient value

Total revenue from patients who first attended twelve to eighteen months ago, averaged. Aggregated industry data puts med spa patient value somewhere in the $1,800–$5,200 range over twelve months, but the spread is wide enough that only your own figure is useful for planning.

5. Rebooking rate within the treatment window

Of patients who had a treatment with a natural repeat cycle, what share returned within the appropriate window. Track it by treatment type — a blended average across neurotoxin, filler and laser hides exactly the segment that is failing.

6. Response time to new enquiries

Time from enquiry to first meaningful reply, measured across all hours and all channels. Business-hours-only measurement produces a flattering number that describes a fraction of your demand.

7. Unconverted consultation pool

How many patients attended a consultation in the past 90 days without purchasing, and what that group is worth at your first-year patient value. This is the number that turns a vague sense of leakage into a decision about whether to do something.

The ones to ignore

  • Social media followers — no reliable relationship to bookings
  • Website traffic in isolation — useful only alongside enquiry conversion
  • Cost per lead on its own — see number three
  • Total calls handled — activity, not outcome
  • Messages sent — the same problem, one channel down

Measure a baseline before you change anything

Whatever you decide to improve, record where it started. Without a baseline, any improvement is a claim rather than a result — which is also the standard you should hold any vendor to when they tell you what they achieved for you.

Key takeaways

  • Show rate and consultation conversion are the two numbers most worth watching
  • Track cost per acquired patient, not cost per lead
  • Calculate first-year patient value from your own data, not an industry range
  • Rebooking rate must be split by treatment type to be useful
  • Record a baseline before changing anything, or improvement is unprovable
