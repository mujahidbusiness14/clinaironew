Building a Med Spa Follow-Up System That Doesn't Annoy Patients

There are two failure modes in aesthetic follow-up. The first is doing nothing, which is common. The second is sending everyone the same discount three days later, which is worse, because it trains your patients to wait for it.

Start with why she hesitated

A patient who left because of price needs different information from one who left because her sister's wedding is in April. Treating both to the same message means at best one of them ignores it and at worst you discount a sale that was never about money.

The practical version is a four-way split recorded by the provider at the end of the consultation: booked, thinking it over, price concern, or timing. Four buttons. Ten seconds. Everything downstream depends on it.

What each objection actually needs

  • Uncertainty — before-and-after results for her specific treatment and face shape, and reassurance about how conservative the provider's approach is
  • Price — financing options, package or membership pricing, and clarity about what the number actually covers
  • Timing — a follow-up dated to the thing she mentioned, not a generic nudge in two weeks
  • No clear objection — one useful message, then leave her alone

Never lead with a discount

A discount in the first follow-up message solves a problem you have not diagnosed and creates one you will keep. Patients talk to each other, and a practice known for discounting after consultations will find that more consultations end in 'let me think about it' — because thinking about it is now profitable.

If price is the genuine barrier, financing and package structure address it without devaluing the treatment. Save any actual discount for a specific, time-bound reason, and never as the opening move.

Timing that respects the patient

Same day is too soon; it reads as pressure. Two weeks is too late; she has either booked elsewhere or forgotten the conversation. A sensible shape is a first message within a day or two carrying genuinely useful information, a second around day four that references the specific consultation, and a human handover around day seven to ten for high-value cases that have gone quiet.

Everything stops the moment she replies. That single rule prevents most of the damage automated follow-up can do, and it means your team only ever sees warm conversations rather than a queue of silence.

Write like your front desk, not like marketing

The message should reference the provider she saw, the treatment discussed and the date she came in. That specificity is what separates a follow-up from a broadcast. 'Hi Marisa, Dr. Chen mentioned you were weighing up lip filler on Tuesday' lands completely differently from 'Still thinking about your treatment?'

The compliance layer nobody mentions

Follow-up to patients in the United States is outbound contact, and that carries obligations: documented consent, contact-hour limits by the recipient's time zone, immediate opt-out across every channel, and suppression lists that persist. Text is considerably simpler ground than automated voice, which is one reason to keep recovery on messaging and route anything that needs a call to an actual person.

Measure recovery, not activity

Messages sent is a vanity number. The metric that matters is consultations recovered — patients marked unconverted who subsequently booked, traced back to the original consultation. If your system cannot draw that line, you cannot tell whether the follow-up worked or whether those patients would have returned anyway.

Key takeaways

  • Split unconverted consultations by objection before writing a single message
  • Never open with a discount — it trains patients to hesitate on purpose
  • Day one, day four, and a human handover around day seven works better than a fortnight of silence
  • Stop the sequence the moment she replies
  • Track consultations recovered, not messages sent
